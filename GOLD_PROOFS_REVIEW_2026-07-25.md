# 8. The “Painted-On” Creature Problem — Coding / Art Pipeline Recommendations

This is the single most useful area to address if you want the team to stop hearing “it looks spray-painted on.”

## Why it currently reads painted-on

Right now, many surface treatments appear to be driven primarily by:

- albedo striping
- highlight bands
- top-surface decorative strokes
- very limited silhouette change
- weak attachment shading

So the viewer sees **a base body with a texture added**, instead of **a body made out of fur/scales/feathers**.

## What to do from a coding standpoint

## A. Move surface generation from “overlay texture” to “structural surface layer”

Instead of painting a pattern on top of the base body, generate a **surface field** tied to the creature’s form.

### Recommended pipeline

1. Build the final body silhouette first.
2. Compute a **signed distance field (SDF)** from that silhouette.
3. Derive a **normal/tangent field** from the SDF.
4. Generate fur/scales/feathers along those tangent directions.
5. Let the surface type affect:
   - silhouette edge
   - local shadowing
   - highlight breakup
   - contour rhythm
   - overlap/attachment occlusion

That one change will make the material feel grown from the body rather than painted onto it.

---

## B. For fur: affect the silhouette, not just the fill

### Current issue
Fur often looks like top-lit banding or short stripes.

### Better approach
- Create a **fur depth mask** near the outer contour.
- Add **irregular silhouette fuzz** using noise-modulated edge dilation.
- Use 2–4 fur length classes:
  - plush
  - short coat
  - shaggy
  - mane/tuft
- Use clustered directional tufts, not evenly spaced lines.
- Add subtle **self-shadow bands** under thicker tufts.

### Rule of thumb
If a creature is furred, the outer contour should change at least a little. If the contour stays perfectly smooth, it will always read like paint.

---

## C. For scales and plates: use repeated geometry logic, not stripes

### Current issue
Scaled creatures often look like a smooth body with decorative marks.

### Better approach
- Seed scale anchors using jittered grids or poisson-disk spacing.
- Warp the anchors through the body’s tangent field.
- Render each scale as a small overlapping form with:
  - top highlight
  - lower shadow
  - slight occlusion at overlap edge
- Scale size should vary by zone:
  - smaller on limbs/neck
  - larger on torso/back
- Let dorsal ridges or belly plating switch sub-patterns.

### Visual result
The body starts to read as assembled from scale plates rather than skinned with a printed texture.

---

## D. For feathers: separate body fluff from wing feathers

### Current issue
Feathered creatures often read like fur with sharper strokes.

### Better approach
Use **three feather systems**:

1. **Contour fluff** — tiny feather breakup on body silhouette  
2. **Body feathers** — short directional grouped marks  
3. **Flight feathers** — long organized layered forms on wings and tail

Body feathers should follow local flow; wing feathers should follow a real arm/hand structure.

---

## E. For wings: build them from anatomy, not decals

This is the second-biggest area after surface materials.

### Current issue
Wings often look like added side-shapes or decorative flaps.

### Better approach
Define a real wing rig:

- **shoulder anchor**
- **upper arm**
- **forearm**
- **hand / primary span**
- **membrane OR feather fan**

### Rendering order
1. draw body
2. draw rear wing behind torso
3. draw shoulder occlusion/contact shadow
4. draw front wing in front when applicable
5. draw feather or membrane substructure
6. add lighting transition at the shoulder insertion

### Important rule
The wing root must visually disappear *into* the torso with shadow and overlap. If the root looks like it was pasted on the outside edge, the player will read it as fake.

---

## F. Use attachment occlusion everywhere

Accessories and traits should have **contact shadows** where they meet the body:

- wings to torso
- horns to skull
- plates to back
- fins to body
- spines to shell
- frills to neck

A tiny soft darkening pass at each attachment point goes a very long way.

---

## G. Add “material-specific silhouette budgets”

Give each surface type a small controlled silhouette influence:

- scaled: 1–2 px rhythm changes
- furred: 2–6 px fuzz/tuft variation
- feathered: 2–8 px plume or vane breakup
- plated: chunkier stepped contour
- crystalline: faceted corner interruptions
- chitinous: segmented rim accents

This will immediately stop different materials from all sharing the same smooth blob outline.

---

## H. Use zone maps on the creature body

Split the body into zones such as:

- head
- neck
- torso
- belly
- limb upper
- limb lower
- dorsal line
- tail

Then let the material generator respond by zone.

Example:

- tiger stripes strong on torso/tail, weaker on lower limb
- scales denser on back, larger on flank
- fur tuft longer on neck and tail tip
- feathers concentrated at shoulders and hips

This makes the creature feel biologically organized rather than uniformly painted.

---

## I. Preserve stylization while increasing naturalism

You do **not** need to move toward realism. The goal is:

- keep the simplified silhouette language
- keep the clean rendering style
- keep the legibility
- but make the materials feel native to the form

In other words: **stylized anatomy, not sticker textures**.
